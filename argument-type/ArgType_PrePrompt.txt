The relevant argument types are as follows.
1. TEXT
These arguments rely on the ordinary meaning of the text of the relevant legal norm (whether that be a statute, a constitutional provision, or an established common-law formulation). They may include explicit discussion of the meaning of those words. However they also include simple statements of the norm that claim that the facts do (or do not) fall under its terms, or claims about the “clear” or “explicit” terms of the norm. The key feature of these arguments is that they appeal to the words used, treating these as capable of resolving the dispute.
2. PURPOSE
These arguments focus on the purpose or goal of a given norm (whether that be a statute, a constitutional provision, or an established common-law formulation). They argue that a particular outcome reflects what the legislator (in the case of a statute) or other author intended (or should have intended) to achieve through the relevant norm. They also include arguments about the policy implications of a particular interpretation or decision, or the substantive (un)fairness or (in)justice of the outcome, whether for the parties to the dispute or others who are similarly situated.
3. SYSTEMIC
These arguments link the legal issue in dispute to broader features of the relevant legal regime, or of the legal system as a whole. They include arguments pointing to the wider statutory context, drawing parallels or contrasts with other statutory provisions, or highlighting how different norms interact. They also include arguments that appeal to established principles of law to clarify the matter in dispute, and arguments drawing analogies to other norms or principles in order to clarify and apply the norms in dispute.
4. PRECEDENT
These arguments focus on the ways that relevant norms have been established or interpreted in past decisions in order to argue that the same approach should be taken in the present dispute. This may include reference to specific named past decisions, or more general reference to the way questions have been resolved by courts in the past.
